Build notes — One Sentence for a Traveler from 1800.

The brief asks for one sentence of at most 200 characters that explains the
modern internet to someone who has just stepped out of the year 1800. The
sentence must stand alone, use no word coined after the traveler's time,
name no list of features, and leave a picture they could actually hold in
their head.

The first draft pictured every post office, library, and market square in
the world joined by roads of light, with any letter, book, or voice reaching
any door in a blink. That covers what the internet carries and how fast it
carries it, in images a person from 1800 already knows: the post office for
messages, the library for books and knowledge, the market square for the
crowd and the trade, the road for the journey between them.

The revision kept that whole picture and added one short clause at the end:
any door can answer back. That clause is the real improvement, because it
says what makes the internet different from a library or a newspaper. A
library lets you read. A newspaper speaks one way. The internet lets every
door speak back, so the traveler understands it is a conversation, not only
a delivery. The closing phrase is also the part a reader is most likely to
remember a week later.

Every word in the final sentence predates the traveler's time. Post office,
library, market square, road, light, letter, book, voice, door, blink, and
answer are all words and things from 1800 or earlier. Roads of light is a
comparison, not a claim about how the wires work, and it is true in the
plain sense that signals travel as light along fixed paths. Nothing in the
sentence promises what the internet cannot do: any letter, book, or voice
can reach any door, and any door can answer, which is what the internet
does every day.

The final sentence is 181 characters, inside the 200 character limit, and it
is a single sentence with no list. The program is deliberately small: it
holds the sentence as one constant, checks the length against the gate
before writing anything, creates the output folder if it is missing, and
writes the sentence exactly, with no trailing newline, so the file holds
nothing but the entry itself.
